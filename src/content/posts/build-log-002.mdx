In the [last dispatch](/blog/build-log-001/) — four days ago — I made a bet out loud: that a decade of building software in public creates more leverage than a decade of excellent work no one can see. It cashed almost immediately.

A company I know needed a real tool — and I built it.

The relationship came through my network, not an inbound form. But the bet from the last post still held up: the work I'd already put in public is what made the conversation easy. I could show what I'd built instead of describing it.

## The problem

The business is a 24/7 industrial equipment-rental company on the Gulf Coast — the kind that keeps refineries and construction sites running with lifts, compressors, generators, and pumps, around the clock, across multiple branches.

The problem was the one that's always the problem: the people closest to the work were running the business out of a spreadsheet and a stack of paper forms. Reservations, service calls, what's on rent, what's coming back — all of it lived in someone's head and a file that only opened on a desktop. The single most valuable number in the business — *how much equipment is on rent right now* — took real effort to answer.

So I built them a live rental board.

## What I built

It's a passcode-gated, mobile-first web app in their own brand. Open it on a phone and the first thing you see is the number that matters: dollars and units on rent, returns due, open service calls. Their two real daily forms — the rental reservation and the service request — are now a few taps that save to a database and produce the exact text they already send. I loaded their real catalog so it speaks their language out of the box, not a generic demo.

The feature list isn't the point. The person running the branch now opens one screen and *sees the business*, instead of reconstructing it every morning.

## How it got built

The visible app is the easy part to talk about — and it's not where the work is. The hard part is up front and unglamorous: sitting with how the business actually runs, loading their real catalog, and getting the data model right so the tool reflects reality instead of a generic template. I work the same way I do on everything here: alongside Claude Code, with a real engineering setup around it. I write a tight spec, review and validate every output, and deploy and own the result. The AI does the mechanical lifting; the judgment is mine.

## What this proves (and what it doesn't)

The way I build — alongside AI, shipping with a real address — turns a messy real-world process into working software a business can actually run on. That's the harder bar.

What it doesn't prove yet is repeatability. One build is an anecdote. The work now is to turn this into a before-and-after I can point to, and to do it again for the next process worth killing.
